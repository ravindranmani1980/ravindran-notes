How cloud cost works

Pay-as-you-go versus buying hardware, the default-plan trap, and why you can resize at any time.

Moving to the cloud changes how infrastructure is paid for, not just where it runs. Instead of buying servers every few years and hoping they last, you rent capacity by the second or hour and get an invoice every month. That is a big advantage, but only if someone keeps an eye on what is running and why. This guide explains the basics that the rest of the FinOps guides build on. The examples cover Azure, AWS and Google Cloud side by side.

No hardware to buy up front

In a data centre, capacity is a capital purchase. You size servers for the peak you expect over the next three to five years, pay for them on day one, and live with the result. If you guessed too big, the money is already spent. If you guessed too small, you wait weeks for new hardware.

In the cloud there is no up-front purchase. You pay for what you provision, for as long as it exists. That gives you three things a data centre can't:

  • Start small. Begin with a modest size and grow when real usage tells you to, instead of buying for a guess.
  • Resize at any time. Moving a VM to a bigger or smaller size is usually a restart, not a hardware project. The decision you make today isn't locked in.
  • Stop paying when you stop using. Delete a test environment on Friday and its cost stops on Friday. Power off a VM at night and you stop paying for its compute.

The catch is the other side of the same coin: nothing stops paying on its own. A VM that nobody uses still bills every hour, and a disk left behind after a server is deleted bills every month. Cloud cost is something you manage continuously, not once a year.

Pay-as-you-go is the default plan

Every new account starts on pay-as-you-go: list price, billed per second or hour, no commitment. It's the most flexible way to pay and also the most expensive per hour. That's fine while you learn how a workload behaves. Once usage is steady, the same resources can cost a lot less with a commitment (see guide 05).

The default-plan trap

Portals and templates are designed to work first time, not to be cheap. The defaults are often bigger, faster or more redundant than a workload needs, and nobody goes back to change them. Common examples:

The FinOps loop

FinOps is the practice of making cloud spend visible, owned and optimised, together with finance. The FinOps Foundation describes it as three repeating phases. In plain terms:

  1. Inform: see where the money goes, by team, application and environment. This needs consistent tags or labels (see the Azure, AWS and Google Cloud guides).
  2. Optimise: remove waste, right-size, schedule non-production, and commit to the steady baseline.
  3. Operate: make it routine: budgets, alerts, a monthly review, and cheaper defaults in templates.

The guides in this section follow that loop: measure (02), right-size (03), schedule (04), commit (05), stop the leaks (06) and review every month (07).

Who owns the bill

Cost optimisation fails when everyone assumes someone else is watching. Give every subscription, account or project an owner, tag every resource with an owner and cost centre, and send each owner their own numbers every month. Engineers make the changes; finance sees the trend; the owner decides what is worth paying for.
